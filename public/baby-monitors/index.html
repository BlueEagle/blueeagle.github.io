Collin's Code

For a bigger screen, the VAVA 8-inch is the sharpest. The VTech HQ Max is the value pick.

Three of these four are closed-loop, non-Wi-Fi systems: the camera talks only to its own parent unit over a dedicated 2.4 GHz radio, with no app, account or cloud. That removes the attack surface behind nearly every real baby-monitor hack. The fourth, the Babysense Touch, adds Wi-Fi and an app. It has a local mode with no internet, but it still carries a Wi-Fi radio. Bigger screens mostly mean softer pictures, so the real trade-off is size against sharpness.

Reviews and company trust

A monitor runs all night next to a lithium battery and your baby, so who stands behind it matters. Here's what we found on each maker.

VTech

Most trusted here
VTech HQ Max and VM7468HQ

Hong Kong's VTech Holdings, founded in 1976, says it is the largest maker of home phones in the US. It has a real security black mark: in 2015 a SQL-injection breach of its kids' Learning Lodge service exposed about 5 million parent and child accounts. The 2018 FTC settlement cost $650,000 and requires audited security for 20 years. That breach involved its connected toys. These two monitors have no app, account or cloud, and we found no CPSC recalls for them.

Babysense (Hisense Ltd.)

Mixed: good products, recent recall
Babysense Touch 7"

Hisense Ltd. is an Israeli company, not the TV maker. It has made breathing-movement monitors since the early 1990s. Its non-Wi-Fi Babysense PRO is among the best-reviewed monitors anywhere (4.9★ from 465 Babylist reviews). In February 2026 the CPSC recalled about 81,800 Max View parent units that can overheat or spark while charging, with 11 incidents reported. Trustpilot reviewers say replacements have been slow, and the UK site scores 2.2/5. The Touch isn't part of that recall, but it's a new battery-powered unit from the same company.

VAVA (Sunvalley Group)

Lowest trust
VAVA IH020

VAVA is a brand of Shenzhen's Sunvalley Group, alongside RAVPower and TaoTronics. In June 2021 Amazon removed all three after a Wall Street Journal report that they traded gift cards for reviews, and a class action followed. That makes glowing star counts on its own site and on Amazon hard to trust. Its Trustpilot score is 1.2/5 from 74 reviews, mostly about units failing after a few months and slow warranty help. Retailer ratings are better (4.5★ on Babylist, 4.2★ on Walmart), and it has a 12-month warranty with a 30-day trial.

Trustpilot scores reflect few reviews and lean negative for most hardware brands, so read them as a signal about support, not product quality. Retailer star ratings are per product and include incentivized reviews on some sites.

Why cloud monitors keep failing

In May 2026, researcher Sammy Azdoufal found flaws in Meari's shared backend, which powers Amazon brands like Arenti, Boifun and ieGeek. More than a million cameras were affected. One bug, CVE-2026-33359, left motion-alert images on Alibaba OSS with no auth or expiry. Another used a hardcoded signing key that ships in every Meari-based app. One backend bug hits every customer at once.

Where "hack-proof" is marketing

Frequency hopping isn't encryption, and none of these vendors publish their cipher, key exchange or pairing protocol. A neighbor with a wideband SDR and patience could in principle reverse-engineer a link. Researchers did that to Chamberlain's FHSS garage sensor, and cheap analog 2.4 GHz cameras have been sniffed from a passing car. A Wi-Fi-based local link is a better-studied target with off-the-shelf tools. Still, any targeted RF attack on your street is a far smaller threat than a bulk cloud breach.

Setup checklist for any of them

  • Buy new and sealed, not open-box or used. Then pair the camera to the parent unit yourself, so no unknown unit is linked.
  • Check the camera list on the parent unit now and then. Only your own cameras should show.
  • Mount the camera at least 3 ft from the crib and keep cords out of reach. That's standard AAP/CPSC guidance and matters more than any radio threat.
  • With the Babysense Touch, leave the app off unless you need it. If you turn it on, put the camera on its own IoT network, keep its firmware updated, and pull any microSD card before you sell or return it.
  • Remember the camera has two-way talk. It transmits audio whenever talk-back is on, so keep that off when you aren't using it.
  • If you later want phone viewing, skip vendor clouds. Use a PoE or RTSP camera on an isolated IoT VLAN, record locally (Frigate or similar) and reach it over WireGuard. That way you own every hop.
